Repeated-Measures ANOVA — the same people, measured again

Lecture 8 · Course 2.1 Methods & Statistics · Bruno Bocanegra · Erasmus University Rotterdam

A note before you start

Sezen — this lecture adds exactly one structural idea to everything you already know, and it is worth naming before the arithmetic starts. In a one-factor ANOVA the differences between people sit inside the error term, anonymous and unmeasured. Here the same people appear in every condition, so those differences can be identified, measured, and lifted out of the error before the test is run. That is the whole lecture. The F for the situations is then computed against a much smaller yardstick, and effects that a one-factor ANOVA would miss become visible.

You run this design at work constantly without calling it that. Measuring the same cohort of managers before a programme, three months after, and a year after is repeated measures; comparing three different cohorts is not. The difference matters for the same reason it matters here: when you follow the same people, each person is their own control, and you stop paying for the fact that some managers simply rate everything high. The price is an order problem — the second survey is never the first survey — and the fix is the same as the lecture’s: vary the order, or accept that time and treatment are tangled.

  • Recognise a repeated-measures design from a description, by counting the scores against the people.
  • Explain why several scores from one person are not independent, and what that breaks.
  • Identify an order effect in a described study, and say whether it confounds the condition.
  • Explain what counterbalancing does, what it does not fix, and what it costs.
  • Name the four kinds of repeated measurement — stimulus, task, situation and time — and give an example of each.

🏛️ The argument in one paragraph

A repeated-measures ANOVA is the one-factor ANOVA you already know, run on a design in which the same people appear in every condition — and that one change pays for itself twice. Because each person is measured in all k conditions, each person has a mean of her own, and the spread of those person means around the grand mean is a quantity you can compute: SS Between persons. It is lifted out of the total first and never tested against the conditions. What remains, SS Within persons, splits a second time into the part the conditions explain, SS Model, and the part they do not, SS Error — which is exactly the amount by which the individual profiles fail to be parallel, and is formally the person × situation interaction, which is why its df is the product (n − 1)(k − 1). Both F values are then divided by one shared MS Error. In the lecture’s study that yields F(2, 6) = 9.60 for the situations, .01 < P < .05, with R² = .48, and a causal reading is allowed because the situation was imposed and the order counterbalanced. The gain is not rhetorical: the same twelve scores analysed as three independent groups give MS error 3.778 instead of 1.667, and F = 4.24 against a critical value of 4.26 — not significant. The person differences were always there; the repeated-measures design is simply the one that refuses to call them noise. The price is an order effect, which counterbalancing breaks the link to but does not remove, and the sphericity assumption, which Mauchly tests and Greenhouse-Geisser or Huynh-Feldt patch by shrinking the degrees of freedom while leaving the F untouched.

🧭 Where this sits — the same people, measured again

One score per person, or several — the question that splits the family

Every test you have met so far compared DIFFERENT people: one group against another, three groups against each other. This lecture is the other branch of the family tree. Here the same people are measured again under every condition, and that single change in the design changes what counts as noise.

  • 🏷️A between-subjects design divides people over conditions — one score per person. Independent-groups t test, one-way ANOVA, two-factor ANOVA.
  • 👥A within-subjects design measures each person under every condition — several scores per person. Paired-samples t test, and now repeated-measures ANOVA.
  • 🔁The independent variable is still qualitative: conditions, situations, moments. The dependent variable is still quantitative.
  • 🧮Two conditions and the same people → paired t test. Three or more conditions and the same people → repeated-measures ANOVA. That is the whole of this lecture.
  • ⚙️The tell in a stem: count the scores against the people. 12 scores from 4 people is within-subjects; 12 scores from 12 people is between.
🪝 Memory hook
Different people → between. Same people again → within. Count the scores against the people.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Last week I compared three groups of different people. This week it is the same four people measured in three situations — so each person acts as their own comparison, and I have to handle that in the analysis.”
★ Key insight
The design decides the analysis before any number appears. If the same person supplies more than one score, those scores are not independent, and an analysis that assumes they are will put the person differences into the error term where they do not belong.
🧪 Demo 1 — between-subjects, repeated measures, or neither?
Pick one. You get the reasoning either way.

A researcher measures reaction time in 30 people under three noise levels. Between or within?
Within-subjects — 30 people but 90 scores. The same people are measured three times, so repeated-measures ANOVA.
And if she had used 90 people, 30 per noise level?
Between-subjects, one score each — an ordinary one-way ANOVA on df (2, 87).

They are the same idea at two sizes. The paired t test is the two-condition case; repeated-measures ANOVA takes it to three or more, exactly as one-way ANOVA took the independent t test to three or more.
Lecture 2 of 16 · story · slides 5–7

💔 The warm-up — and what went wrong with it

A paired t test with a problem hiding inside it

The lecture opens with a within-subjects study that reaches the wrong conclusion, and it is worth sitting with, because the fault is in the design and not the arithmetic. Six love-struck students hold a heated rod — mild pain — while looking at a picture of either their crush or an acquaintance, and a brain scan gives a pain score.

  • 🔥Research question: do you feel less pain when in love? Independent variable: the picture (crush or acquaintance), manipulated. Dependent: pain score from the scan.
  • 📉H₀: µCrush = µAcquaint. Hₐ: the two differ.
  • ⏱️Every student saw the acquaintance first and the crush second. Mean pain 19.67 against 15.50, a mean difference of 4.17, and the paired t test returns P = .01.
  • 🚫So: looking at your crush hurts less? No. By the second picture every student had been holding the rod longer and was more used to the heat.
  • ⚠️Being used to the stimulus rides along with the condition. It is a confounder: it changes with the independent variable, so the two explanations cannot be told apart.
🪝 Memory hook
A significant P-value cannot rescue a confounded design. Order was perfectly tangled with condition.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“They always showed the acquaintance first and the crush second, so by the time the crush came up everyone had got used to the heat. The pain went down, but we cannot tell whether that was love or just time.”
★ Key insight
In a within-subjects design the danger is not that people differ — it is that the ORDER of the conditions is itself a variable. Here order was held constant for everyone, which is the worst case: it makes the confounder perfectly correlated with the condition.
🎬 Watch: The warm-up🎬 Watch: a repeated-measures example worked by hand
🧩 Comprehension check
Name the three variables in this study, and which is the problem.
Independent: the picture, crush or acquaintance. Dependent: the pain score. Confounder: being used to the stimulus, which increases with time and therefore with the condition order.
The P-value was .01. Does that mean something real was found?
Something real moved the scores, yes — but the test cannot say whether it was the picture or the habituation. A P-value measures evidence against H₀; it says nothing about which of two tangled explanations is doing the work.
Would more participants have fixed it?
No. A confound is not noise. Running 600 students in the same fixed order would produce the same bias with a smaller P-value.
Lecture 3 of 16 · method · slides 8–9

🔁 Counterbalancing — the fix, and what it costs

Break the link between order and condition

The fix is to break the link between condition and order by varying the order across participants. Half the students see the acquaintance first, half see the crush first. Habituation still happens — it just stops lining up with the condition, so it lands in the noise instead of in the effect.

  • 🔀Participants 1–3 ran acquaintance then crush; participants 4–6 ran crush then acquaintance. Same six people, same measurements, different order.
  • ⚖️The differences (acquaintance − crush) are now 3, 5, 0, 0, −2, 4. Mean difference 1.67, sdiff 2.73.
  • 📏t = 1.67 ⁄ (2.73 ⁄ √6) = 1.49 on df = 6 − 1 = 5, which puts P between .10 and .20. H₀ is not rejected.
  • ✅The apparent effect was mostly habituation. With the order counterbalanced the effect shrinks from 4.17 to 1.67 and stops being significant.
  • 💸Counterbalancing is the standard protection in experimental within-subjects work — and in observational repeated measures it is not available at all, which is Remark 2 in Lesson 16.
🪝 Memory hook
Counterbalancing does not remove the order effect. It spreads it evenly so it cannot masquerade as the condition.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“We flipped the order for half the group. Now whatever getting-used-to-it does, it does to both conditions equally — so it cannot pretend to be the effect of the picture any more.”
★ Key insight
Compare the two analyses of the same study: P = .01 with a fixed order, P between .10 and .20 once the order is counterbalanced. The design, not the statistics, decided the answer.
🎬 Watch: Counterbalancing🎬 Watch: a repeated-measures example worked by hand
🧩 Comprehension check
Why does the mean difference change from 4.17 to 1.67?
Because in the counterbalanced half the habituation now works AGAINST the crush condition rather than for it. Averaged over both orders most of the habituation cancels, and what is left is the picture effect.
Three conditions instead of two — how many orders are there?
Six (3 × 2 × 1). Full counterbalancing gets expensive fast, which is why a Latin square or a randomised order per participant is often used instead.
Does counterbalancing make the design experimental?
It is part of what makes it experimental: the researcher controls the condition and randomises the order. An observational repeated-measures study — measuring the same children at four ages — cannot randomise time.
Lecture 4 of 16 · orientation · slides 11–14

🗂️ What repeated measures looks like in the wild

Stimulus, task, situation, time — four ways the same unit gets measured again

Before the arithmetic, a feel for the shape. Repeated measures turns up whenever the same unit is measured more than once, and the slides give several examples that look quite different from each other but share one structure: a factor whose levels are all visited by the same people.

  • ☕Stimulus: seeing coffee, smelling coffee, “hearing” coffee → how much do you crave coffee? Three measures on each person.
  • ✏️Task: a calculus test, a language test, a writing test, a drawing test → the level of each student. Four measures on each student.
  • 🏠Situation: at home, at school, on the street → the problem behaviour of a child.
  • 📅Time: intake, 3 months, 6 months, after a year → the same child’s behaviour at home. This is longitudinal research.
  • 🔬The slides group these as three sub-types: experimental (conditions the researcher imposes), observational (several measures on the same scale), and longitudinal (repeated measurements over time).
🪝 Memory hook
Conditions, tasks, situations, moments in time — if the same unit visits all of them, it is repeated measures.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“It is not only before-and-after studies. Four different tests on the same students counts too, because every student gives me four scores.”
★ Key insight
The word “condition” is doing a lot of work. It can be something done to the participant, something they are asked about, or simply a moment in time — the analysis does not care, as long as every unit supplies a score at every level.
🎬 Watch: What repeated measures looks like in the wild🎬 Watch: a repeated-measures example worked by hand
🧩 Comprehension check
A survey asks each employee how important it is to be appreciated by their boss, their colleagues, and their customers. Repeated measures?
Yes — observational repeated measures. Three measures on the same scale from each employee, so the three scores are linked and cannot be treated as independent.
Which of these sub-types cannot be counterbalanced?
Longitudinal. You cannot randomise which measurement comes first when the levels ARE the time points.
Is “which of four tests did a student score highest on” a between or within question?
Within. The comparison is made inside each student, which is exactly the information a between-subjects design throws away.

🏙️ The study — fear in three situations

Four women with agoraphobia, three situations each

The running example for the rest of the lecture. A study into agoraphobia measures the fear score of four participants in three situations. Small enough to compute entirely by hand, which is the point: every sum of squares in this lecture can be checked on twelve numbers.

  • 🎯Participants: Ans, Belinda, Carla, Dorien. Situations: a written description of an open space, video images of an open space, and actually being in an open space.
  • 📐Dependent variable: fear score. Quantitative, interval. Three measurements per person.
  • 🧍Independent variable: situation. Qualitative (a factor), three levels, and its domain is within-subject.
  • 🎲n = 4 persons, k = 3 situations, N = 4 × 3 = 12 observations — and only 4 people.
  • ⚗️Level of control: experimental. The order in which people meet the three situations is randomised — the lecture’s word for this is counterbalancing, although four people cannot fill all six orders — so a causal reading will be available.
🪝 Memory hook
4 people, 3 situations, 12 scores. Write n, k and N down before anything else.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Four people each did all three things — read about an open space, watched a video of one, then stood in one — and we measured how frightened they were each time.”
★ Key insight
Naming the design completely (dependent variable and its level, independent variable and its levels, and the DOMAIN — within or between) is the step that determines every degree of freedom later. The slides spend a whole page on it for that reason.

What makes the situation factor “within-subject”?
Every participant is measured at every level of it. Nobody is assigned to just one situation.

🔢 The data, and the one extra column that changes everything

Twelve scores, and the person mean that makes them paired

Here is the whole data set. Twelve fear scores in a grid: one row per person, one column per situation. The lecture then adds a column that an independent-groups analysis could never compute — the person mean — and that column is the entire advantage of the design.

  • 🔢Ans 6, 6, 9 → person mean 7. Belinda 7, 9, 11 → 9. Carla 4, 9, 8 → 7. Dorien 3, 4, 8 → 5.
  • ➡️Situation means: Text 5, Video 7, Real 9. Grand mean 7.
  • ⬇️Obviously you have means and variation per situation — 3.33, 6.00 and 2.00. But now you also have means and variation across all situations, person by person: the person means vary with a variance of 2.67.
  • 🎯Belinda is a frightened person in every situation; Dorien is a calm one in every situation. That is a real, measurable fact about these data, and a between-subjects design cannot see it.
  • 🔑Read the grid twice: down the columns for the situation effect, across the rows for the person effect.
🪝 Memory hook
Two directions in one grid. Down the columns = the situations. Across the rows = the people.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Each row is one person’s three scores, so I can see who is a jumpy person overall. Each column is one situation, so I can see which situation frightens everybody. Both are in the same little table.”
★ Key insight
The person mean is the new information. Every formula in the next six lessons is just a question about how far something sits from one of these three means — the person mean, the situation mean, or the grand mean.

🧩 Comprehension check
Compute Carla’s person mean and say what it tells you.
(4 + 9 + 8) ⁄ 3 = 7. Carla sits exactly at the grand mean of 7, so she is an average-fear person overall — even though her three individual scores vary a lot.
The grand mean is 7. Is that the mean of the four person means, or of the three situation means, or of the twelve scores?
All three — (7 + 9 + 7 + 5)⁄4 = 7, (5 + 7 + 9)⁄3 = 7, and the twelve scores also average 7. With equal numbers in every cell the three routes agree, which is a useful check.
Why can an independent-groups design not compute a person mean?
Because each person supplies only one score, so there is nothing to average within a person. The person-to-person differences are still there — they just cannot be separated from the error.
Lecture 7 of 16 · hypotheses · slides 21

0️⃣ Two sets of hypotheses, not one

Down the columns and across the rows — two null hypotheses

Because the grid can be read in two directions, there are two null hypotheses — one about the situations and one about the persons. The slides write both out. Only the first is usually the research question, but both get an F in the finished table.

  • 0️⃣With respect to Situation: H₀: µText = µVideo = µReal. Hₐ: these means are not all equal.
  • 0️⃣With respect to Persons: H₀: µAns = µBelinda = µCarla = µDorien. Hₐ: these means are not all equal.
  • 🚫Hₐ is “not all equal” in both cases — never “all differ”, and never directional. There is no one-sided F test.
  • 🧍The hypotheses are about population means (µ), not about the sample means you can already see in the grid.
  • 🎯The situation hypothesis is the research question. The person hypothesis is almost never interesting — of course people differ — but testing it costs nothing and the slides report it.
🪝 Memory hook
Two H₀’s: one down the columns, one across the rows. Both say “all equal”; both Hₐ’s say “not all equal”.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“There are two questions in the same table. Do the three situations differ? And do the four people differ? The first is what the study is about; the second is almost certainly yes.”
★ Key insight
This is the structural difference from one-way ANOVA. There, person differences were anonymous and sat inside the error term. Here they have a name, a hypothesis and a row of their own — and that is precisely why the error term shrinks.
🎬 Watch: Two sets of hypotheses, not one🎬 Watch: a repeated-measures example worked by hand
🧩 Comprehension check
Write Hₐ for the situation test in words.
“The mean fear scores in the three situations are not all equal in the population.” Not “all three differ”, and not “Real is highest”.
Why is the person H₀ nearly always rejected?
Because people genuinely do differ in baseline fear, and the design is built to detect exactly that. It is a sanity check, not a finding.
Could you test whether Real is scarier than Text with this F?
No. F squares the deviations, so it carries no direction. A specific pair needs a pairwise comparison, which Lesson 16 gets from SPSS with a Bonferroni adjustment.

✂️ Splitting the variation — twice, not once

The total splits twice: persons out first, then the test

This is the hinge of the whole lecture. In a one-factor ANOVA the total variation splits once, into between-groups and within-groups. In a repeated-measures ANOVA it splits twice, and the second split is where the test lives.

  • ✂️One-factor ANOVA: SSTotal → SSBetween groups + SSWithin groups. One split, and the between part is the effect.
  • 🧍Repeated measures, first split: SSTotal → SSBetween persons + SSWithin persons.
  • 🔁Second split, on the within-persons part only: SSWithin persons → SSM (the model — the situations) + SSR (the residual — noise).
  • 🔒So the between-persons part is lifted out before the test is built. It is not the effect and it is not the error; it is removed.
  • 📉The F for the situations is SSM against SSR — both of them pieces of the within-person variation. The person differences never enter that comparison.
🪝 Memory hook
Total → between persons + within persons. Then within persons → situations + noise. Two cuts, and the second one is the test.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“First I separate how much people differ from each other. Then, inside what is left, I separate how much the situations differ from pure noise. The second comparison is the actual test.”
★ Key insight
Everything good about this design follows from the first cut. By naming the person variation and setting it aside, the yardstick that the situation effect is judged against becomes much smaller — which is why four participants can produce a significant result.
🎬 Watch: Splitting the variation🎬 Watch: a repeated-measures example worked by hand
🧩 Comprehension check
Which sum of squares is the denominator of the situation F built from?
SSR, the residual — the leftover of the WITHIN-person variation after the situation effect has been taken out of it. Not the between-persons row.
In a one-way ANOVA on the same twelve scores, where would the person differences go?
Straight into SS within groups, the error term — inflating it, because a one-way analysis has no way to tell a person difference from noise.
Why is the second split applied only to the within-persons part?
Because the situations are a within-person factor: every person visits all three, so the situation effect shows up as movement INSIDE each person. There is nothing about situations in the between-persons part.
Lecture 9 of 16 · compute · slides 24–27

🧮 SS Total, and SS Between persons

Every score against the grand mean, then every person mean against it

Now the arithmetic, on twelve numbers. Two sums of squares, each one a question about distance from a mean. Both are computed the same way as every sum of squares you have ever met: take a deviation, square it, add them up.

  • 🧮SSTotal — the variation of the twelve individual scores around the grand mean of 7: (6−7)² + (6−7)² + (9−7)² + (7−7)² + (9−7)² + … = 66.
  • 🎯dfTotal = N − 1 = 12 − 1 = 11.
  • 🧍SSBetween persons — the variation of the four person means around the grand mean. Each person mean stands for 3 scores, so each squared deviation is multiplied by k = 3.
  • ✖️3 × (7−7)² + 3 × (9−7)² + 3 × (7−7)² + 3 × (5−7)² = 3 × (0 + 4 + 0 + 4) = 24.
  • ✅dfBetween = number of persons − 1 = 4 − 1 = 3.
🪝 Memory hook
SS Total: every score against the grand mean. SS Between persons: every PERSON MEAN against the grand mean, times k.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“For the between-person bit I pretend each person just has their average score, three times over, and ask how far those averages sit from the overall average.”
★ Key insight
The × 3 is not decoration. A person mean summarises three observations, so it carries three observations’ worth of weight. Forget it and SS between persons comes out three times too small — 8 instead of 24 — and all 16 of the missing units land in the error, which goes from 10 to 26.

Why is Ans’s contribution to SS between persons zero?

N − 1 = 11. Note that it is built on the twelve OBSERVATIONS, not the four people — the commonest slip in this lecture.

📥 SS Within persons, and splitting it again

Subtract, then split what is left

The within-persons part is what is left of the total once the person differences are out: it is the movement of each person’s own scores around their own mean. The lecture gets it by subtraction and then confirms it directly, and then splits it into the situation effect and the noise.

  • ➖SSW = SST − SSB = 66 − 24 = 42, and dfW = dfT − dfB = 11 − 3 = 8.
  • ⬇️Directly: the variation of each person’s scores around his or her own mean — (6−7)² + (6−7)² + (9−7)² + … + (8−5)² = 42. Same answer, two routes.
  • 🧮dfW counted the other way: each person contributes k − 1 = 2, and there are 4 people, so (3 − 1) × 4 = 8.
  • 🔁SSM — the variation of the three situation means around the grand mean, each standing for n = 4 scores: 4 × (5−7)² + 4 × (7−7)² + 4 × (9−7)² = 32, with dfM = k − 1 = 2.
  • 📏SSR = SSW − SSM = 42 − 32 = 10, and dfR = dfW − dfM = 8 − 2 = 6.
🪝 Memory hook
SS W = 42 by subtraction or directly. Then 42 splits into 32 for the situations and 10 for the noise.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Take out how much people differ from each other and 42 is left. Of that 42, thirty-two is the three situations pulling everybody up or down together, and only ten is people wobbling in their own private way.”
★ Key insight
Note how big the situation effect is relative to what is left: 32 against 10. That ratio — once each is divided by its own degrees of freedom — IS the F test. Everything up to here has been bookkeeping to get those two numbers.

Compute SS M and say in words what it measures.
4 × (5−7)² + 4 × (7−7)² + 4 × (9−7)² = 16 + 0 + 16 = 32. It measures how far the three situation means sit from the grand mean, weighted by the four people behind each one.

Both SS W and SS R can be found by subtraction. What is the check?
Compute one of them directly as well. SS W is 42 either way, and SS R comes out as 10 both by 42 − 32 and by summing the squared observed-minus-expected gaps in the next lesson.

🎯 Expected scores — where the error term actually comes from

Person mean plus situation mean minus grand mean

SSR arrived by subtraction, which tells you its size but not what it means. The slides then build it from scratch, and this is the most illuminating page in the lecture: for every cell of the grid there is a score the model expects, and the residual is what the data did instead.

  • 📐The rule: expected = person mean + situation mean − grand mean.
  • 📊Ans is an average-fear person (her mean is 7, the grand mean). Text is 2 points below the grand mean. So we expect 7 + 5 − 7 = 5 from Ans in the Text condition.
  • 📏Belinda sits 2 points above average overall, and Text is 2 points below, so the two cancel: 9 + 5 − 7 = 7.
  • 🔀The full expected grid is 5 / 7 / 9 for Ans and Carla, 7 / 9 / 11 for Belinda, 3 / 5 / 7 for Dorien — every row the same shape, just shifted up or down by the person.
  • 🧩SSR = Σ(observed − expected)² = (6−5)² + (6−7)² + (9−9)² + … + (8−7)² = 10. The same 10 as the subtraction gave.
🪝 Memory hook
Expected = your own average, adjusted by what this situation does to everybody. The residual is the bit neither explains.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“The model says: you are a jumpy person, so add two; and this is the scary situation, so add two more. Anything left over after that is noise.”
★ Key insight
The expected grid is the model’s picture of the world: every person has the same situation profile, raised or lowered by their own level. Plotted, that is a set of perfectly PARALLEL lines. The residual is exactly the amount by which the real lines fail to be parallel — Carla crossing the others is the biggest single contribution.

Compute the expected score for Dorien in the Real situation.
5 + 9 − 7 = 7. Her observed score was 8, so her residual is +1.
Ans scored 6 in the Text condition and the model expected 5. What caused the extra point?
Nothing the model knows about — it is noise. That one point contributes 1² = 1 to SSR.
Why does the expected grid have parallel rows?
Because it is built by adding a person effect to a situation effect, with no term allowing the situation effect to differ between people. If the real data were perfectly parallel, SSR would be zero.

📐 Degrees of freedom — the four rows

Everything is minus one — and the error df is a product

The degrees of freedom follow the same tree as the sums of squares, and the slides point out that they behave like the df in a two-factor ANOVA. Get these four right and the table completes itself.

  • 1️⃣dfTotal = N − 1 = 11. dfBetween persons = n − 1 = 3.
  • 🧍dfWithin persons = dfT − dfB = 8 — or, counting directly, (k − 1) per person × n persons = 2 × 4 = 8.
  • 🔁dfM = k − 1 = 2. dfR = dfW − dfM = 8 − 2 = 6.
  • ✖️The residual df can also be written (n − 1)(k − 1) = 3 × 2 = 6, which is the two-factor interaction rule — the error term IS the person × situation interaction.
  • ✅Check: 3 + 2 + 6 = 11 = dfTotal. The df column adds down the table exactly as the SS column does.

df R = (n − 1)(k − 1). MULTIPLY. And the three effect df must add to N − 1.

“Three for the people, two for the situations, six left over for the noise — and three plus two plus six is eleven, which is twelve scores minus one.”

Writing dfR as (n − 1)(k − 1) explains what the error term is: the extent to which the situation effect differs from person to person. It is an interaction between person and situation, and with one score per cell there is nothing else it could be.

📋 The finished table — two F values

Two F values, one shared denominator

Every piece is now in hand. The slides assemble them into the repeated-measures ANOVA table, and they make one thing explicit that is easy to get wrong: both F values are divided by the SAME mean square, the noise.

  • ➗The four sums of squares and nothing else: situation 32, person 24, noise 10 → total 66. “These are all the sources of variance.”
  • 🎯MS = SS ⁄ df, row by row: person 24 ⁄ 3 = 8.00; situation 32 ⁄ 2 = 16.00; noise 10 ⁄ 6 = 1.667.
  • 🧍For the F values you use MSnoise. Situation: 16.00 ⁄ 1.667 = 9.60 on (2, 6). Person: 8.00 ⁄ 1.667 = 4.80 on (3, 6).
  • 📏Both P-values fall between .01 and .05 — exactly, F(2,6) = 9.60 gives P = .013 and F(3,6) = 4.80 gives P = .049.
  • ✍️R² per row is that row’s SS over the total: situation 32 ⁄ 66 = 0.48, person 24 ⁄ 66 = 0.36, noise 10 ⁄ 66 = 0.15.
🪝 Memory hook
Two F’s, one denominator. MS noise is the yardstick for both.

⚖️ The verdict, the effect size, and the causal claim

Reject, size it, and say what the design lets you claim

Two tests, two decisions, and then the question the whole design was built to license: may we say the situation CAUSED the difference in fear? Here, unusually, the answer is yes — and it is the design that says so, not the P-value.

  • ✅Situation: H₀ is rejected (P &lt; .05). The fear scores in the population are not the same in the three situations. The effect is strong, R² = .48.
  • ✅Person: H₀ is rejected (P &lt; .05). There are significant differences between the persons. The effect is strong, R² = .36.
  • 📏What the F does not say: which situations differ, or in which direction. The sample means run 5, 7, 9, but naming a pair needs a pairwise comparison.
  • ⚗️Causality. This was an experiment — the situation was imposed and the order randomised — so the causal interpretation is unique: the situation a person is in affects their fear score.
  • 🚫Contrast Lesson 2: the same machinery on a confounded design produced a significant P-value and a false conclusion. The statistics did not change; the design did.
🪝 Memory hook
Significant F → the means are not all equal. Which ones, and why, are separate questions, and only the design answers “why”.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“Fear really does depend on the situation — reading about an open space, watching one, and standing in one are not the same. And because we randomised the order and controlled the situation, I can say the situation caused it.”
★ Key insight
Keep “significant” and “strong” apart. P &lt; .05 says the pattern is unlikely to be chance; R² = .48 says how much of the variation it accounts for. With four participants, a strong effect is the only kind this study could have detected.

💪 Why bother — what the pairing actually buys

The same twelve numbers, analysed two ways

The slides now ask the obvious question: what would have happened with twelve different people instead of four measured three times? The answer explains why this design is worth the trouble, and it is the most exam-relevant idea in the lecture.

  • ⚡Imagine 12 different people: 4 tested with Text, 4 with Video, 4 with Real. Gerda scores 9 in the Video condition. Why is that score so high — because of Video, or because of Gerda? You cannot tell.
  • 👥With repeated measures the scores are linked: each participant is his or her own control. Gerda’s 9 can be compared with Gerda’s other two scores.
  • 🔇That gives additional information. Nobody here finds Video less frightening than Text — three women rise and one stays level. In a between-subjects design, measuring each person once, you could not see that at all.
  • 💪Using that information lets you test more reliably: the person differences leave the error term, so the yardstick shrinks.
  • ⚠️Which is why a significant result can appear despite a very small sample — the power is larger. Four participants reached P = .013.
🪝 Memory hook
Each person is their own control. That is where the extra power comes from — a smaller denominator, not a bigger effect.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“With different people I would never know whether a high score meant the condition was scary or the person was jumpy. Measuring the same people, I can see each person move — and everyone moved the same way.”
★ Key insight
Put the two analyses side by side on these twelve numbers. Repeated measures: MS error 1.667, F = 9.60, P = .013. The same twelve numbers as three independent groups of four: the person variation of 24 goes back into the error, giving MS error (24 + 10) ⁄ 9 = 3.78 and F = 16.00 ⁄ 3.78 = 4.24, P = .051 — not significant. Same means, opposite verdict.

✅ Conditions, the SPSS output, and the three remarks

Sphericity, Mauchly, and reading the SPSS output

Last, what has to be true for the test to be trusted, how SPSS lays it out, and the three caveats the lecture closes on. This is the most examinable page in the lecture after the df.

  • 📋Normality: the residuals are normally distributed, where residual = score − expected score (Lesson 11).
  • 🔵Sphericity: varianceA−B ≈ varianceB−C ≈ varianceA−C — the variances of all the pairwise DIFFERENCES are about equal. It is tested by Mauchly’s test, where H₀ is “sphericity is ok”.
  • 🧪Here Mauchly gives W = .360, χ² = 2.043 on 2 df, P = .36. H₀ is not rejected, so the RM-ANOVA provides useful results and the “Sphericity Assumed” row is the one to read: F(2, 6) = 9.60, P = .013.
  • 🛠️If sphericity fails, SPSS offers corrected rows that multiply both df by an epsilon: Greenhouse-Geisser (ε = .610 → df 1.22 and 3.66, P = .039), Huynh-Feldt (ε = .808 → P = .023), lower bound (ε = .500 → P = .053). F never changes — only the df and so the P-value.
  • 🗣️Pairwise comparisons with a Bonferroni adjustment: Text against Real differ (mean difference 4.00, P = .007); Text–Video and Video–Real do not (P = .483 each).
🪝 Memory hook
Mauchly first. If P > .05, read “Sphericity Assumed”. If not, read Greenhouse-Geisser — same F, smaller df, bigger P.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend
“First SPSS checks whether the three pairs of differences wobble by about the same amount. Mauchly does not reject, so I read the top row — though with only four people that is weak reassurance rather than proof. If it had rejected, I would read the Greenhouse-Geisser row, where the F is the same but the P is bigger.”
★ Key insight
The three closing remarks: (1) the design is used often — efficient with limited data, practical for experiments, and change within persons is itself interesting; it often appears combined with a between-subjects factor as a mixed design. (2) in observational research counterbalancing is impossible, so watch for autonomous maturing and spontaneous recovery. (3) “subject” need not mean “person” — it means the unit of interest, which might be a family interviewed as eldest, middle and youngest child.

🏛️ Synthesis — the whole lecture in one paragraph

A repeated-measures ANOVA is the one-factor ANOVA you already know, run on a design in which the same people appear in every condition — and that one change pays for itself twice. Because each person is measured in all k conditions, each person has a mean of her own, and the spread of those person means around the grand mean is a quantity you can compute: SS Between persons. It is lifted out of the total first and never tested against the conditions. What remains, SS Within persons, splits a second time into the part the conditions explain, SS Model, and the part they do not, SS Error — which is exactly the amount by which the individual profiles fail to be parallel, and is formally the person × situation interaction, which is why its df is the product (n − 1)(k − 1). Both F values are then divided by one shared MS Error. In the lecture’s study that yields F(2, 6) = 9.60 for the situations, .01 < P < .05, with R² = .48, and a causal reading is allowed because the situation was imposed and the order counterbalanced. The gain is not rhetorical: the same twelve scores analysed as three independent groups give MS error 3.778 instead of 1.667, and F = 4.24 against a critical value of 4.26 — not significant. The person differences were always there; the repeated-measures design is simply the one that refuses to call them noise. The price is an order effect, which counterbalancing breaks the link to but does not remove, and the sphericity assumption, which Mauchly tests and Greenhouse-Geisser or Huynh-Feldt patch by shrinking the degrees of freedom while leaving the F untouched.

  • c.Three — the two main effects and the interaction
  • d.Two — that the condition means are all equal, and that the variances of the differences are equal
The grid is read in both directions. Down the columns: H₀ that the condition population means are all equal, which is the research question. Across the rows: H₀ that the person population means are all equal, which is the sanity check and is almost always rejected.
The other options
✗a. The person effect gets a row of its own in the table and a hypothesis of its own. That is precisely what is new in this design.
✗c. There is one factor here, not two. The interaction term exists but it is the error term; it is not tested.
✗d. Sphericity is a CONDITION of the analysis, tested separately by Mauchly. It is not one of the hypotheses the ANOVA tests.
🏷️ Two directions in the grid, two null hypotheses. Only the column one answers the research question.
Question 7 · lecture 8
In a repeated-measures ANOVA, which two quantities does the F for the conditions compare?
  • a.SS Between persons against SS Within persons, the two branches of the first split
  • b.MS Model against MS Between persons, the two named effects in the finished table
  • c.MS Between persons against MS Total, scaled by the total degrees of freedom
  • d.MS Model against MS Error, both of them parts of the within-person variation
The total splits twice. The person differences are lifted out first and never tested against the conditions. The F for the conditions lives entirely inside the second split: MS Model against MS Error, both pieces of the within-person variation.
The other options
✗a. Those are the two branches of the FIRST split, and they are never set against each other.
✗b. MS Between persons is the numerator of the other F, not a denominator for this one.
✗c. There is no MS Total — mean squares never add up down the table, so a total one would mean nothing.
🏷️ Draw the two-stage tree before you compute anything. The F you are asked for always lives in the second branch.
Question 8 · lecture 10
A repeated-measures analysis gives SS Total = 66 and SS Between persons = 24. The three situation means are 5, 7 and 9, with four persons and a grand mean of 7. What is SS Error?
  • a.10
  • b.32
  • c.42
  • d.24
SS Within persons is 66 − 24 = 42. SS Model is n × Σ (situation mean − grand mean)² = 4 × [(5−7)² + (7−7)² + (9−7)²] = 4 × 8 = 32. SS Error is what is left of the within-person part: 42 − 32 = 10.
The other options
✗b. That is SS Model, the part the situations explain — the error is what remains after it is removed.
✗c. That is SS Within persons, which still contains the model part.
✗d. That is SS Between persons, which was taken out in the first split and is not part of the error at all.
🏷️ Subtract twice, in order: total minus persons, then that minus the model. The second remainder is the error.
Question 9 · lecture 11
Carla’s person mean is 7, the Text situation mean is 5, and the grand mean is 7. Carla actually scored 4 in the Text condition. How much does that cell contribute to SS Error?
  • a.9, because (7 − 4)² = 9
  • b.4, because (4 − 7 + 5)² = 4
  • c.1
  • d.0, because her person mean equals the grand mean
The expected score is person mean + situation mean − grand mean = 7 + 5 − 7 = 5. The observed score is 4, so the residual is −1 and the contribution is (4 − 5)² = 1.
The other options
✗a. That compares her score with her own person mean, which ignores the situation entirely. The expected value is built from both margins.
✗b. That arranges the three means in the wrong order. The grand mean is subtracted once, not added.
✗d. Her person mean equalling the grand mean only means the situation mean alone sets her expected score. It does not make the residual zero.
🏷️ Expected = row mean + column mean − grand mean. The grand mean is subtracted because it is otherwise counted twice.
Question 10 · lecture 12
Twelve participants are each measured in four conditions. What is the degrees of freedom for the error term?
  • a.44
  • b.33
  • c.36
  • d.11
df Error = (n − 1)(k − 1) = 11 × 3 = 33. It is a product because the error term is formally the person × condition interaction, and interaction degrees of freedom are always the product of the two main-effect df.
The other options
✗a. That is df Total, N − 1 = 48 − 1 = 47 — and 44 is neither. The error df is smaller than both.
✗c. That is df Within persons, (k − 1) × n = 3 × 12 = 36, which still contains the model df.
✗d. That is df Between persons, n − 1 = 11.
🏷️ A product, not a sum. If you ever write df Error as a difference, check it against (n − 1)(k − 1) before you divide.
Question 11 · lecture 13
A repeated-measures table shows SS Model = 32 on 2 df and SS Error = 10 on 6 df. What is F for the model?
  • a.3.20
  • b.0.31
  • c.9.60
  • d.19.20
MS Model = 32 ⁄ 2 = 16.00 and MS Error = 10 ⁄ 6 = 1.667. F = 16.00 ⁄ 1.667 = 9.60.
The other options
✗a. That is SS Model divided by SS Error, 32 ⁄ 10 — the sums of squares, not the mean squares.
✗b. That is the ratio the other way up. F for an effect always has the effect’s MS on top.
✗d. That divides MS Model by SS Error ⁄ df Model, or 16 ⁄ 0.83 — the denominator must use the ERROR degrees of freedom.
🏷️ Always convert both sums of squares to mean squares first. An F built from raw SS is the single commonest slip in this topic.

Question 13 · lecture 13
For F(2, 6) = 9.60, the critical values are 5.14 at the .05 level and 10.92 at the .01 level. What should the report say?

  • b..01 < P < .05, so H₀ is rejected
  • c.P < .01, so H₀ is rejected
  • d.P = .05 exactly, so the result is inconclusive
F = 9.60 beats the .05 critical value of 5.14, so H₀ is rejected. It does not reach the .01 critical value of 10.92, so the P-value lies between the two: .01 < P < .05.

Question 14 · lecture 14
A repeated-measures ANOVA on a counterbalanced experiment gives F(2, 6) = 9.60, .01 < P < .05, with situation means of 5, 7 and 9. Which conclusion is justified?
  • a.The real place causes significantly more fear than the video does, since 9 beats 7
  • b.All three situations differ significantly from one another, because F is significant
  • c.The situation affects the fear reported, but naming which situations differ needs a pairwise comparison
  • d.Nothing causal may be claimed here, because only four people took part in the study
A significant F rejects the hypothesis that the three population means are all equal. Because the situation was imposed and the order counterbalanced, the reading is causal. What the F cannot do is name a pair — the sample means run 5, 7 and 9, but testing any one gap requires a pairwise comparison.
The other options
✗a. Naming a specific pair needs a pairwise comparison. In this study the Video-Real gap in fact has a Bonferroni P of .48.
✗b. Hₐ in an ANOVA is “not all equal”, never “all different”. Only one gap here survives correction.
✗d. A small sample costs power, not causal licence. The licence comes from manipulation and randomised order, both of which are present.
🏷️ A significant F licenses the sentence “the factor matters”, never the sentence “these two levels differ”.
Question 15 · lecture 15
The lecture’s twelve fear scores analysed as a repeated-measures design give F(2, 6) = 9.60. Analysed as three independent groups of four, the same twelve scores give F(2, 9) = 4.24. What changed?
  • a.SS Model grew, because pairing the scores makes the situation effect itself larger
  • b.The error degrees of freedom grew from 6 to 9, and that is what raised the F value
  • c.Both the numerator and the denominator of the F ratio changed between the analyses
  • d.Only the denominator: the 24 units of person variation went back into the error term
MS Model is 16.00 in both analyses — the situation effect is identical. Treating the scores as independent returns the 24 units of person variation to the error, which becomes 34 on 9 df, so MS Error rises from 1.667 to 3.778 and F falls to 4.24 — just short of the critical 4.26.
The other options
✗a. SS Model is 32 in both analyses. Pairing never changes the size of the effect.
✗b. The error df actually rises from 6 to 9, which on its own would make rejection EASIER. F still fell, because the mean square rose much more.
✗c. The numerator is untouched: MS Model = 16.00 either way.
🏷️ Repeated measures does not find a bigger effect. It finds a smaller yardstick.
Question 16 · lecture 16
What exactly does the sphericity condition require?
  • a.That the variances of the scores in the three conditions are equal
  • b.That the variances of all the pairwise differences between conditions are equal
  • c.That the person means are normally distributed
  • d.That each person’s scores are independent of one another
Sphericity is about the DIFFERENCES, not the conditions. It requires var(Text − Video), var(Video − Real) and var(Text − Real) to be equal — which is the repeated-measures replacement for the equal-variances condition of a between-subjects ANOVA.
The other options
✗a. That is the equal-variances condition of a between-subjects ANOVA. Here it is replaced, not reused.
✗c. Normality is a separate condition, and it applies to the scores in each condition.

Question 17 · lecture 16
Mauchly’s test returns W = .360, χ²(2) = 2.04, P = .36. Which row of the SPSS output should be read, and why?
  • a.Sphericity Assumed, because H₀ for Mauchly is that sphericity holds and a P of .36 does not reject it
  • b.Greenhouse-Geisser, because a P of .36 is larger than .05 and the assumption has therefore failed
  • c.Lower-bound, because it is the safest row whenever n is small
  • d.Any of them, because the F value is the same in all four rows
H₀ for Mauchly is “sphericity holds”. P = .36 is not small, so H₀ stands and the uncorrected Sphericity Assumed row is the one to read. Note that the logic runs the opposite way round from most tests: here you WANT a large P.
The other options
✗b. A large P supports H₀, which here is the assumption itself. The assumption has not failed.
✗c. The lower bound is the most conservative correction, but it is applied when sphericity fails, not whenever n is small.
✗d. The F is indeed identical in all four rows — but the degrees of freedom are not, so the P-values differ and the row you pick decides the verdict.
🏷️ Mauchly is the one test where a big P is good news. Its H₀ is the assumption you want to keep.
Question 18 · lecture 16
A Greenhouse-Geisser correction with ε = .610 is applied to F(2, 6) = 9.60. What happens?
  • a.F falls to 5.86, because F is multiplied by ε
  • b.F rises, because the degrees of freedom shrink
  • c.F stays at 9.60; the degrees of freedom become 1.22 and 3.66, so the P-value rises
  • d.Both F and the degrees of freedom are multiplied by ε, leaving the P-value unchanged
A sphericity correction multiplies both degrees of freedom by ε and leaves F alone: 2 × .610 = 1.22 and 6 × .610 = 3.66. Fewer degrees of freedom means a higher critical value, so the same F now carries a larger P — here .013 becomes .039.
The other options
✗a. F is never rescaled. Only the degrees of freedom are.
✗b. F does not move at all, in either direction.
✗d. If the P-value were unchanged the correction would be pointless. Shrinking the df is exactly what makes the test more conservative.
🏷️ ε touches the df and nothing else. If your answer changes F, it is wrong.
